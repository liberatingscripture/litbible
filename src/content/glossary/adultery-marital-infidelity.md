The English word "adultery" has come to be associated with sexual shame, which is not the primary focus of the Greek. *Moicheia* focused on first-century legal status and the violation of a legal marriage agreement. It applied only to a husband being wronged, because he was seen as the holder of the agreement. If two unmarried people had sex, it was not *moicheia*. If a married man had sex with an unmarried woman, it was not *moicheia*. It was *moicheia* technically only when a man was wronged because his exclusive rights to his wife had been breached. That legal shape is what makes Jesus' use of it in the gospels striking. He takes the category and turns it around, holding a husband accountable for betraying his wife by sending her away. In that culture, a woman sent away had no means of providing for herself and was generally condemned to destitution, so dismissing her was not a neutral legal act. It was an abdication of responsibility, and Jesus names it as the offense. The rest of the New Testament also seems to use it that same unique way, holding men accountable to honoring their responsibility to their wives, in a departure from how the word was used in other Greek literature. In any case, the word itself is not about sex. It is about the violation of a marriage agreement and the responsibilities involved in that covenantal arrangement. With that in view, there are places where it is used to refer to a breach of a covenant agreement between God and the people, framing betrayal of the covenant oath from Deuteronomy 26:17–18 as equivalent to violation of a marriage agreement.
