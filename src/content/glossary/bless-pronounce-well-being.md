*Eulogeo* is literally to "speak well," and it is used in three basic ways. First, the simple use is to praise someone, giving thanks or affirmation of the good things they do or provide. Second, it can be used to refer to promised good things or gifts, something good that was spoken about in advance. Third, it functioned as a speech-act, a benediction, a "speaking well" over someone and pronouncing a future state of well-being or good fortune. The English word 'bless' uses lofty language to convey often trite things people are pleased about or are bragging about in a socially acceptable way (#blessed). In the Southern region of the United States, it's often used passive aggressively to insult someone with polite language ("bless your heart"). The Hebrew word that informed the tradition in the Hebrew Bible, *barach*, works the same way as eulogeo, and neither word is related in origin or meaning to the one traditionally translated 'blessed' in the Beatitudes, which is *makarios* and has an entirely different usage. See the entry for "Blessed" ("how greatly fortunate") for that one. When the crowd shouts as Jesus enters Jerusalem, they are praising the one who comes representing the Lord. When Jesus takes bread and *eulogeo* happens, he is speaking praise as an expression of thanks, not performing a miracle on the bread.
