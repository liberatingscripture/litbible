*Pantokrator* is traditionally translated 'Almighty,' following the Latin *omnipotens*. The Greek compound more literally means "the one who holds all," from *pas* ('all') and *krateo* ('to hold, sustain, or keep firm'). While it can convey strength or might, its core imagery is not domination but upholding, sustaining, and preserving all things. In the Septuagint, *pantokrator* is used to translate both *YHWH Tsevaot* and *El Shaddai*. The Hebrew *tsevaot*, traditionally rendered 'hosts,' refers to multitudes, forces, or ordered gatherings; rather than implying militarism, it often refers to the heavenly assembly, the vastness of creation, or the gathered people of God. The title *El Shaddai* has uncertain etymology. Older scholarship commonly linked it to *shadad*, meaning 'to overpower or destroy,' reinforcing translations like "God Almighty," but more recent scholarship increasingly favors derivations from *shad*, meaning 'breast,' conveying nurture, provision, and motherly care. In the book of Job, *pantokrator* regularly appears where the Hebrew reads *Shaddai*, reinforcing a divine identity grounded in faithful presence and sustaining power, not coercion. Translating it as 'Sustainer of All' preserves the scope of divine power while emphasizing God's role as the one who upholds, nourishes, and faithfully holds all things in being. It resists coercive or domination-based associations and affirms a theology of power grounded in presence, care, and abundance.
