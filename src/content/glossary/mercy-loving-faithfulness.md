The English word "mercy" is sometimes used to communicate compassion, which is what is in view here. But "mercy" is also used for "pity" or for "relenting from enacting a punishment," and that has nothing to do with this Greek word. *Eleos* is used of taking care of people. It is derived from the word *elaion*, "olive oil," which was used as a medicinal ointment in caring for the sick. The related word *eleēmosunē* refers the act of charitable giving, or alms-giving. It's translated in Matthew 6:2 in the LIT as "compassionate giving." The Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, uses *eleos* to translate the Hebrew word *hesed*. That is a theologically significant word that has been rendered several ways: "lovingkindness," "loyal love," "covenant faithfulness," "committed love," and sometimes just "love." It carries a firm commitment to pursue good for others that is grounded in care. While sometimes used of humans, such as Ruth for Naomi (Ruth 1) and Boaz for Ruth (Ruth 2), it is most often used of God, of God's faithful love toward God's people, staying present with them and honoring the work of providing and protecting and caring for them whether or not they reciprocate. Empathy is related but only part of what is in view. Compassion is related but only part of what is in view. Help is related but only part of what is in view. No single English word sufficiently carries the full meaning, which is why the phrases "loving faithfulness" and "committed compassion" have been used to attempt to carry a fuller sense of the meaning.
