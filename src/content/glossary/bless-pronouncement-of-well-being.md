*Eulogeo* is literally to speak well, and it does two related things in these texts: it praises someone, and it speaks good over someone for the time ahead. Neither of those is quite what English "bless" now conveys, which tends toward a vague religious favor that descends on a person from above, or toward the polite noise made after a sneeze. The Hebrew word behind most of its uses, *barach*, works the same way, and it is worth being clear that neither word is related in origin or meaning to the word translated "blessed" in the Beatitudes, which is *makarios* and is a different claim entirely. When the crowd shouts at Jesus coming into Jerusalem, they are pronouncing well-being over the one who comes representing the Lord. When Jesus takes bread and *eulogeo* happens, he is speaking praise, not performing a rite on the loaf. LIT renders it as speaking praise or pronouncing well-being according to which of the two the sentence is doing, and renders the opposite movement, wishing harm, in matching terms, so that a reader can see the pair.
