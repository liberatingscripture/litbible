This word is rendered as "submit" or "be subject to" in most translations, including in passages that have been used to demand obedience from wives, from enslaved people, and from anyone with less power in a relationship. However, the word is richer and more interesting than that. *Hupotasso* was a Greek military term meaning to arrange troop divisions under the command of a leader. In non-military use, the *Theological Dictionary of the New Testament* describes it as "a voluntary attitude of giving in, cooperating, assuming responsibility, and carrying a burden." Lexicons note that in non-hierarchical contexts it can denote voluntary alignment, cooperative responsiveness, or living out a commitment a person has already professed. The word sometimes appears in the New Testament near and in contrast to the word *antitasso* which has often been translated as 'resist.' It was also a military term for arraying troops on the field of battle in opposition to an enemy force, and it is appropriate to read *hupotasso* in that military sense in those passages. Where it is related to mutual *hupotasso* or domestic contexts, that military context and the assumed hierarchy with its expectation of absolute obedience is not appropriate. There is one detail in the military sense worth holding onto, however. In some historical contexts, such as the democracy of Athens, the one arranging the troops would take a place among the ranks once everyone was in position, as a co-participant in the battle rather than as someone standing over it. In that way, taking on the role of organizer or manager coordinating a cooperative effort is something worth *hupotasso* (cooperating with) while acknowledging that it is a temporary and situationally specific arrangement and not something inherent and permanent based on gender or socioeconomic status.
